## Land area description

Remote, and accessible only by air and water, Ontario's largest and most northerly park features a vast, truly wild low-lying tundra landscape. Sub-arctic conditions prevail in the park, which is the domain of woodland caribou, moose, marten, fox, timber wolf, beaver, goose, black bear, and polar bear. Seals, walruses, and beluga whales frequent coastal and estuarial areas. Polar bear lumber through coastal areas most notably during July through till December. The peak period is early November. In late spring, hundreds of species of bird descend upon the region. White geese can be seen rising gracefully above the sear barren. Until roughly 4,000 years ago, the mid-Silurian limestone bedrock (450 million years old) here was submerged beneath the Tyrrell Sea, a massive body of water that has retreated into the present Hudson and James bays. No longer burdened by the weight of continental-glaciers, the land is slowly rising at a rate estimated at 1.2 m per century. This is evident in postglacial gravels and sands that mark the raised beaches of ancestral shorelines with the majority of the landscape overlain by a layer of sedimentary clay. The landscape is mostly flat lowlands with extensive inland ridges from the former postglacial shorelines and one upland rock outcrop area east of the Winisk River. Rivers that drain through the park often flood when the ice breaks up in late spring.. Considered the most temperately located mainland tundra in the world the dominant plants on the wetter sites are caribou lichen, reindeer and sphagnum moss while the raised beaches can be quite dry with lapland rhododendron, crowberry, mountain cranberry, species of beach pea and avens. .. The simple plant cover decomposes into the uppermost soil layers; muskeg carpets 80 percent of the terrain, and permafrost occurs within a short distance of the Hudson Bay coast. The treeline encircles the bays like a necklace. North of this invisible limit, few trees grow and they are short in the wind swept landscape. South of the line, stunted willow, spruce and tamarack masquerade as scrubs, gradually rising in height, with distance travelled south. In early summer, the tundra becomes an exquisite heath of mountain avens, fireweed and arctic pea in delirious bloom. Adding to the spectacle, the many ponds that dot the landscape turn rust, yellow, green, turquoise, black, ivory, brown, and other colours, depending on the plant micro-organisms, invertebrates and minerals in the water. Archeologists have determined that Algonquian people lived here perhaps 1000 years ago. Their descendents are the present-day Cree who reside outside the park in the settlements of Peawanuck, Fort Severn, Kashechewan, Fort Albany and Attawapiskat. The residents of Peawanuck, established this community 20 kilometres inland from the Hudson Bay coast on the Winisk River when their former townsite of Winisk was destroyed by catastrophic spring ice breakup and flooding in 1986.Park Facilities and Activities:There are no visitors' facilities. Landing permits and local information must be obtained in advance by persons wanting to access the park. The recent evidence of human habitation in the park is a series of abandoned radar station infrastructure, part of a former Mid-Canada military defence line. The remnants of squat metal buildings, oil tanks, and waste fuel barrels are being cleaned up as part of a joint Federal - Provincial initiative. Four radar dishes and a landing airstrip at site 415 are being left to contine to be used as landmarks and for access to the east side of the park. Visitors to Polar Bear should be prepared for any eventuality in this remote and vast setting. They should bring at least one week's extra supplies in case their departure is delayed due to bad weather. Tents should not rise any higher than necessary, due to the possibility of strong winds. And be aware that this is home to the largest predator in Ontario… the Polar Bear.Location: On the south shore of Hudson Bay, where it comes in contact with the western shore of James Bay, in the far northern area of the province.Polar Bear Provincial Park was regulated in 1970.

## Land use intent

This wilderness class park is designated in regulation under the Provincial Parks and Conservation Reserves Act, 2006. All protected areas within the Far North of Ontario are subject to the provisions of the Far North Act 2010
